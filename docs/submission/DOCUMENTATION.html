Coronary risk you can see on the heart, with a reason behind every number

The problem. In India a primary-care consultation lasts about two minutes on average (Irving et al., BMJ Open 2017): little time to interpret an ECG, an echocardiogram and a page of blood tests, let alone explain them. Only about one in ten Indians speaks English (Census of India 2011), yet most medical reports are written in it. Risk calculators give a single number, but not where the disease is likely to be or why.

Our solution. CardioClarity Studio turns the tests a patient already has into a picture of heart risk: the probability of coronary artery disease (CAD) and of narrowing in each main artery (LAD, LCX, RCA) on an interactive 3D heart, explained input by input and summarised for the patient in English or Hindi. With consent, records are designed to arrive through ABHA, India's digital health ID (93.95 crore accounts by July 2026).

01Predictive performance: data, preprocessing, validation 30%

We use the Extension of the Z-Alizadeh Sani dataset (UCI #411): 303 patients from one Iranian heart centre, all of whom had coronary angiography, with 55 inputs and four labels. Narrowing of 50% or more counts as disease.

Ages 30–86 (mean 58.9); 127 women. Of 216 CAD patients, 86 have one artery narrowed, 67 two and 63 all three. Because always answering “CAD” is 71% accurate, we lead with ROC-AUC, balanced accuracy and F1.

Data cleaning

No missing values and no duplicate rows, so the work was consistency and encoding. The loader rejects any unexpected value instead of filling it in.

  • No label leakage: LAD, LCX, RCA and Cath are labels only, never inputs, as the brief requires; the API rejects them as inputs too.
  • Constant column dropped: “Exertional chest pain” is “No” for every patient.
  • Redundant columns: BMI equals weight / height² exactly and “Obesity” is BMI ≥ 25 (one exception), so only BMI is kept.
  • Merged codes: the mutually exclusive typical, atypical and non-anginal flags become one four-level chest-pain type; current and ex-smoker become one smoking status.
  • Row order: prevalence falls from 80% in the first rows to 33% in the last, so every split is shuffled and stratified.

Feature engineering

A feature was kept only if it raised the average AUC across three untuned models and four targets, and no model lost more than one corrected standard error.

  • Pre-test probability (Diamond–Forrester, from age, sex and pain type): the strongest single feature, univariate AUC 0.83 for CAD.
  • Ten published clinical risk scores (ESC 2019 and 2024, CAD Consortium, Duke, Jang and others), kept where they helped: Duke for LAD, all ten for LCX, Jang for RCA.
  • Wall-motion flags for LAD: abnormal motion in three or more regions meant LAD disease in 28 of 28 patients.
  • Comorbidity count replaces eight rare yes/no columns.
  • Per-target feature selection inside each training fold: 45 inputs for CAD, 53 for LAD, 66 for LCX and 44 for RCA.
  • Linear-model preprocessing per fold: 1st/99th-percentile clipping and log transform of skewed labs.

Validation methodology

  • Fixed folds: 10 repeats × 5 stratified folds (stratified on the CAD and artery pattern), saved once and reused by every experiment, so all comparisons are paired.
  • Choose on repeats 0–4, report on repeats 5–9. Cleaning, features, tuning, model choice, ensemble weights and calibration used repeats 0–4 only. Repeats 5–9 were opened once, for the final numbers.
  • Nested tuning: Optuna searches inside the training folds of repeats 0–2.
  • Paired significance: a difference counts only if it exceeds one Nadeau–Bengio corrected standard error; 95% CIs from a patient bootstrap.
  • Learned inside the fold: scaling, clipping, feature selection, calibration, thresholds and conformal sets are fitted on training patients only. Nothing is fitted on all 303 before splitting.

What we searched

Logistic regression, SVM, kNN, naive Bayes, random forest, extra trees, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost and EBM, all re-tuned; TabPFN v2.5–v3.5 with tuning and fine-tuning; 27 AutoGluon model families with stacking and bagging; joint, hierarchical and chained multi-label models; pooling with an external cohort (AngioCAD, 377 patients). Each target got its own winner by mean AUC on repeats 0–4.

Exploratory findings

  • Chest pain type and age lead for every target.
  • LAD shows on echo: low ejection fraction and wall-motion abnormality (the LAD supplies the front wall).
  • RCA carries an inflammation signal (neutrophils and ESR up); LCX follows age, blood pressure and metabolic factors and is silent on the ECG.

Risk estimation after the model

  • Calibration: Platt scaling, fitted inside each training fold.
  • Consistency: an artery's risk is capped at the CAD risk and flagged only when CAD is, so the heart never shows an artery riskier than any disease at all.
  • Thresholds: per target, for the best balanced accuracy; lower for LCX and RCA, favouring catching disease.
  • Uncertainty: a 90% split-conformal set; when it holds both answers the app marks the result “unsure”.

Study patients are never scored by a model trained on them

A model trained on all 303 patients scores them almost perfectly (AUC 0.99–1.00) because it has memorised their labels. So each target is served as five cross-fitted fold models: a study patient, or a lightly edited what-if of one, is scored by the fold model that never saw them; a new patient gets the average of all five. Scored this way through the live API, the app agrees with the angiogram for 87% of patients on CAD, 81% on LAD and 71% on LCX and RCA, in line with cross-validation.

Uncertainty and artery localisation

The 90% conformal sets hold their coverage (0.889–0.900). For CAD the model commits to one answer for 96% of patients; for LCX and RCA it cannot decide for about half, and shows those as “unsure”. Among CAD patients, picking the involved artery is only slightly better than chance (AUC 0.55–0.62), so the heart shows likelihood of involvement, never a lesion location. Refitting with new seeds moves AUC by at most 0.004.

023D visualization: anatomy, spatial risk mapping and interaction 25%

Anatomical representation

  • Heart: the CC BY 4.0 “Realistic Human Heart” glTF mesh by neshallads (about 22,500 faces), loaded once and reused for the live view and the printed report.
  • Arteries: LAD, LCX and RCA (plus the left main stem) are our own schematic tubes, Catmull–Rom curves laid over the heart's surface in their anatomical territories. Each is a separately addressable object. They show which territory a prediction refers to, not the patient's own vessels.

Spatial risk mapping

  • One colour scale per artery, centred on its own threshold: blue (low) → amber (at the threshold) → red (high). The same scale is printed under the heart and used in every window, so amber always marks the threshold.
  • Risk drives four channels at once: colour, tube thickness, glow and blood-flow speed (instanced particles). Flagged arteries pulse with the heartbeat, which beats at the patient's own pulse rate.
  • Labels on the heart follow each artery and show its percentage, ▲ when flagged and “?” when the model is unsure. While an input is hovered in Why they switch to that input's points for each artery, and arteries it barely moves fade.
  • Live transitions: when a what-if changes the score, every channel eases over 600 ms, so the change in risk is visible on the artery itself.

Interactive visualization

Explore

Orbit and zoom; a depth slider from solid heart through X-ray to arteries only; fly to any artery and back to the overview; labels on or off.

Link

Click an artery on the heart or in the Risk window to select and explain it; hover an input to see its effect on all three arteries at once.

Guide

A guided tour flies through the overall result and each artery. Ctrl+K finds any patient, input, view or window.

Consistent LAD, LCX and RCA correspondence

The heart, the Risk window and the Why window all read the same response object through one artery map, so the colour of an artery and the number beside it can never disagree. Arteries are keyed by the API's target ids, not by position, and the frontend validates every response against the typed contract before anything is drawn.

Accessible 3D: the canvas carries a text summary of all four results for screen readers; reduced-motion settings stop the heartbeat, flow and rotation.

03Clinical interpretability: SHAP explainability, breakdown and what-if 20%

Feature attribution quality (SHAP)

Every response carries SHAP values (Shapley additive explanations) for all four targets, computed with the Baseline Shapley method (32 orderings, against a typical reference patient) on the probability the app actually shows. They add up exactly to this patient's probability minus the typical patient's, so “+51 pts” on the heart means the same as “+51” in the Why window.

We run SHAP on the served probability rather than a model-specific explainer, because the four models differ (a glass-box model, two tree ensembles and a stack): one method gives the same units everywhere and includes calibration and the cap. We chose SHAP over LIME because SHAP values add up exactly and do not change between runs.

Physiological factor breakdown

  • Why window: a journey from the typical patient's risk to this patient's; each row shows the input, the value with its unit, a normal-range bar, a High/Low flag and signed points, largest first. Click an artery to switch every row to it.
  • Factor map: the strongest inputs against all four results at once.
  • Measures: every input as a tile against its typical range, filterable to what needs attention; labs switch between mg/dL and mmol/L.
  • Context: risk among same-age, same-sex study patients, and what the angiogram found.

What-if, in real time

Sliders for the inputs a patient can change (weight, smoking, blood pressure, fasting sugar, triglycerides, LDL, HDL) send a separate request to the same API. The Risk window, Why window and heart show the scenario next to the original, so nothing about the real result is overwritten. A clinician can show a patient what the model would expect if, for example, they stopped smoking.

A what-if shows what the model would predict, not what treatment would achieve.

Clarity of risk explanation for the patient

  • A 3-page report (PDF): the chance with the 3D heart and the main reasons; each artery lit on the heart; take-home numbers, questions for the doctor and when to get help.
  • Listen: a captioned spoken summary of the chance, the arteries, the four main factors, out-of-range readings and a next step.

Decluttered by design

Clinical software often shows every value at once. The interface shows the answer first and detail on request.

  • The heart is the interface. Results live on the anatomy, with small floating windows around it instead of a dashboard of charts.
  • Two windows by default (Patient and Risk). Why opens from “Explain this prediction”; the factor map, measures, comparison and model card open on demand (Ctrl+K).
  • The patient summary lists only abnormal values; the full 49-input profile is one click away.
  • Start screen: four featured patients that each tell a different story, search across all 303, and adding a new patient beside them.
  • One disclaimer line on every screen instead of pop-ups; plain words in the report, no jargon. Two themes, full keyboard use.

One contract, end to end

The API contract (v1.1.0) is typed on both sides: Pydantic in Python and Zod in TypeScript, so a mismatch fails loudly instead of showing a wrong number. Inputs are listed by GET /features with units, ranges and options, and the frontend builds its forms, sliders and tiles from that list. Values outside the training range raise a visible warning.

Patient data intake, designed for India

Entering 49 values by hand is slow, and most already exist in the patient's records. Every route below ends at one review screen, so nothing is scored until a person confirms the values.

  • LIVE Fill in manually, with units, ranges and validation from the contract.
  • DESIGNED Import via ABHA: the patient scans the clinic's QR code with their ABHA app (Scan & Share) and approves consent; labs, diagnoses and vitals arrive as FHIR R4 records through the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission and are mapped onto our 49 inputs.
  • DESIGNED Upload reports: photos, PDFs or WhatsApp forwards of lab, ECG, echo and clinic reports, read by a vision model that shows where each value came from.
  • DESIGNED Speak it in Hindi or another Indian language, transcribed by Sarvam AI speech-to-text and turned into values.
  • DESIGNED Excel / CSV for screening camps: score many patients and rank who to refer first.

05Technical implementation: architecture, code quality, reproducibility 10%

  • Architecture: ml/ (cleaning, features, CV, model search, export), models/ (cross-fitted models and manifest.json), api/ (FastAPI), frontend/ (React 18, TypeScript, React Three Fiber, Zustand, TanStack Query). One Docker image on Cloud Run, 2 vCPU, CPU only.
  • Extensible without a redesign: new inputs come through /features, new models through the manifest, new vessels through the artery map.
  • Code quality: 70 Python tests (data, folds, features, export, API contract, including that study patients are scored by models that never saw them); 163 frontend tests; strict TypeScript and ESLint; automated browser checks at several screen sizes in both themes.
  • Usage: open the live demo, pick a featured patient, press “Explain this prediction”, click an artery, try What-if, open Report or Listen. The Tour walks through everything.
